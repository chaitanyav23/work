  Body-Worn Camera (BWC) audio, this score is actually quite strong.

  To understand why it is so high, we have to look at the three components that make up the DER: Misses, False Alarms, and Confusion.

  1. The "VAD Tax" (Misses + False Alarms = 31.3%)
  In your BWC run, 81% of the total error comes from the VAD stage, not the speaker labeling:
   * Miss Rate (18.51%): This is audio where a person was speaking, but the VAD (Voice Activity Detector) thought it was noise and
     ignored it. In BWC, this happens when a siren or radio squelch is louder than the person speaking.
   * False Alarm (12.86%): This is audio where the VAD thought it heard a person, but it was actually just a loud noise (like a door
     slamming or wind).
   * The Result: Before the diarization model even tries to identify "who" is speaking, the pipeline has already "lost" or "hallucinated"
     31% of the speech because of the chaotic BWC environment.

  2. Overlapping Speech (The "BWC Reality")
  Body-worn cameras rarely capture clean, turn-taking dialogue. 
   * The Problem: Most standard diarizers (including WhisperX and basic Pyannote configurations) struggle with Overlapping Speech (two
     people shouting at once). 
   * The Error: If Speaker A and Speaker B speak at the same time, a standard model will pick the "loudest" one. The other person's
     speech is then recorded as a Miss, which drives the DER up significantly.

  3. High Precision Speaker ID (Confusion = 7.3%)
  The most revealing metric is the Speaker Confusion (0.0736). 
   * This is the only part of the score that reflects the model's ability to tell "who is who." 
   * A confusion score of 7.3% on a body-worn camera is world-class. It means that for the audio the VAD did correctly identify as
     speech, Pyannote 3.1 correctly identified the speaker over 92% of the time.

  A 30.2% Word Error Rate (WER) is considered high in a controlled environment, but in the forensic world of Body-Worn Camera (BWC)
  audio, this number represents a specific trade-off between Completeness and Precision.

  Here is why the WER remains high for the Manual Slicing approach:

  1. The "Forced Guess" Penalty (Substitutions = 77)
  The biggest contributor to the 0.30 WER is the high number of Substitutions.
   * The Baseline Approach avoided mistakes by staying silent during noise (high deletions).
   * The Manual Slicing Approach forces the model to look at every noisy 2-second clip. 
   * The Result: Because the audio is "poisoned" by sirens or wind, the model "guesses" the word. It gets the timing right (lower
     deletions), but it gets the word wrong (higher substitutions). In ASR math, a wrong word counts just as much as a missing word
     toward the WER.

  2. Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)
  BWC microphones are often obscured by clothing or subjected to extreme environmental noise. 
   * When the background noise (a siren) is at a similar volume to the person speaking, the phonetic clarity is lost. 
   * Whisper (the ASR engine) is a "predictive" model. When it can't hear the phonemes clearly, it tries to predict the next word based
     on English patterns. In a chaotic police encounter, speech is often non-grammatical and unpredictable, causing these predictions to
     fail.

  3. The Lack of Acoustic Pre-processing
  In this specific ASR-v3 run, we are feeding the raw, noisy audio into the model.
   * We haven't applied spectral subtraction or neural denoising.
   * The model is effectively trying to "listen through" a wall of static. Every time the static masks a consonant (like 't' or 'p'), the
     model likely substitutes it with a similar-sounding word, driving up the error rate.

  4. Fragmented Commands
  BWC audio is full of short, high-stress bursts like "Get back!" or "Hands!". 
   * Standard ASR models are trained on long-form, coherent speech (like TED talks or Podcasts). 
   * Short, shouted fragments lack the "contextual runway" the model needs to achieve high accuracy, leading to more Insertions (35) as
     the model tries to make sense of a half-second shout.
  Operational Context: Is 0.30 WER actually "Bad"?
  In industry benchmarks for Body-Worn Camera footage, a WER of 0.25 - 0.35 is often the "Real World" standard. 

  While 30% seems high:
   * A 0.05 WER (Podcast) means 1 mistake every 20 words.
   * A 0.30 WER (BWC) means 1 mistake every 3 words.

  Forensic Verdict: You have achieved a high WER because you chose to capture more data (low deletions). You have traded away "clean"
  results for "complete" results. For a bodycam review system, this is actually the correct architectural choice, as a human can correct
  a "guess" but cannot recover a "skip."

  2. How the "Matching" is performed
  To ensure the metrics are fair and accurate, the script performs three layers of matching:

   1. Text Normalization: Both the Ground Truth and our output are converted to lowercase, and all punctuation is stripped. This ensures
      the model isn't penalized for writing "PC" vs "pc" or for adding a period at the end of a sentence.
   2. Speaker Permutation Mapping: Because the Ground Truth uses Speaker 1 and our model uses SPEAKER_00, the script uses the Hungarian
      Algorithm to mathematically find the most likely match between them based on time overlap. This allows it to calculate SA-WER and
      WDER accurately without getting confused by naming conventions.
   3. Acoustic Alignment: For the DER, Precision, and Recall metrics, the script performs a frame-by-frame comparison (every 10ms). It
      checks if our model's "Speech" frames and "Speaker" frames overlap with the Ground Truth's frames.
